1. Abstract 
 
 
This experiment initially involved the synthesis of an iron (III) oxalate complex with the general 
formula Kw[Fex(C2O4)y]·zH2O. The variables x, y, and z were determined through the duration of the 
entire experiment. From 1.2000g of Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 were synthesized 1.1###g of K3[FeIII(C2O4)3]·3H2O, 
owing for a percentage yield of 74.###%. A percentage yield of 11#.##% was also calculated, had the 
final product been K[FeIII(C2O4)2]·2H2O. This value was rejected because experimental errors are never 
to exceed theoretical values, which are taken on assumption that the experiment is carried out in entirely 
ideal environmental settings, which are impossible to attain entirely. In 4B, redox titrations were used to 
calculate the relative composition of oxalate within the iron (III) oxalate salt. The millimoles of oxalate 
were found to be.64#, .63#, .62#, and .62#, for trials A-D, respectively. The weight percentages for each 
trial (A-D) were 54.0#%, 53.4#%, 54.1#%, and 53.##%, with an average of 53.## ± .5###%. It was also 
found that the four trials yielded calculations of 61#, 60#, 61#, and 61# mmol of C2O4
2- per 100g of 
complex analyzed. The averaged value, with the inclusion of two standard deviations, reported to be 61# 
± 7.## mmol of C2O4
2- per 100g of complex analyzed. In 4C, Spectrophotometry was used to determine 
the iron content in the same iron (III) oxalate complex. The average weight percentages of Fe3+ for 
samples 3-5 of the unknowns were found to be 12.##%, 12.7#%, and 14.##%, with an average weight 
percentage of 13.09 ± 2.58%. The number of millimoles present in 100g of complex were found to be, 
for unknown samples 3-5: 215.### mmol Fe3+, 227.### mmol Fe3+, and 259.### mmol Fe3+, with an 
average of 23#.### ±4#.## mmol Fe3+ in every 100g of complex. This data was used, along with the data 
from 4A and 4C, to conclude that the empirical formula of the complex was K3[Fe(C2O4)3]·2H2O, and the 
experiment had a percent yield of 74.##%. 
 
2. Introduction 
 
 
The goal of the three experiments is to successfully synthesize an iron (III) oxalate compound and 
then further analyze it for oxalate (4B) and iron (4C) composition. 
 
 
A coordination compound is comprised of at least one complex ion, and a complex ion itself 
consists of two parts: a [transition] metal ion (which has a positive charge, making it an electron-deficient 
Lewis acid) and one or more ligands (molecules or anions usually of negative charge, rendering them 
electron-rich Lewis bases, through some neutral molecules such as H2O and NH3 work as well). In the 
union of the electron-deficient metal ion and the electron-rich ligand, the two species are said to 
“coordinate” to each other. The coordination number of the metal specifies the number of electron pairs 
which are donated to the metal center. Coordination numbers may vary, with 2, 4, and 6 being the most 
common. 
 
 
Ligands themselves may vary in the number of available binding sites. Monodentate ligands are 
those which donate a single pair of electrons to the metal center. Hence, six monodentate ligands – such 
as H2O- bound to a center metal ion would result in a coordination number of six. “Chelate” and 
“multidentate” are the terms applied to ligands capable of binding at more than one site. Bidentate ligands 
(such as the oxalate ion C2O4
2- used in this experiment) bond at two sites on the center metal ion, and 
tridentate ligands (such as EDTA or ethylenediaminetetraacetato-) bind at three sites. The entire complex 
may have an overall charge that is positive, negative, or even neutral. 
 
 
Reduction-oxidation reactions (or redox, for short) are ones in which there is a change in the 
oxidation numbers of two or more reactant-product pairs. The oxidized component is said to lose 
electrons (i.e., Fe2+ is oxidized to Fe3+), becoming more positively charged, while the reduced component 

 
is said to gain electrons (i.e., Sn4+ gains two electrons to be reduced to Sn2+), becoming less positively 
charged. The redox components occur in pairs, with one species (the reductant) giving up its electron(s) 
to be taken up by the other species (the oxidant). 
 
 
4A involves the synthesis of an iron (III) oxalate complex. The iron is first presented in its Fe2+ 
form, so it must first be oxidized to Fe3+ before the oxalate ion will bind readily to it. Hydrogen peroxide 
is the oxidant of choice: 2Fe2+ (aq) + H2O2 (l) + 2H+ (aq) Æ 2Fe3+ (aq) + 2H2O (l), in acidic solution. (1) 
The oxalate ion is then free to coordinate to the Fe3+ ion, forming a complex of Fe(C2O4). The oxalate ion 
is the conjugate base of the weak oxalic acid, H2C2O4. In lab, careful attention is paid to the maintenance 
of the H2C2O4-C2O4

2-) of the 
salt. An aqueous solution of the iron oxalate salt is prepared for titration, and the oxalate content is found 
through analysis by measuring the volume of titrant (.0100M KMnO4) needed to react completely with 
the known volume of analyte, or the species to be titrated. Here, it is the aqueous solution of the iron 
oxalate salt. The stoichiometric (or equivalence) point is the point at which equivalent stoichiometric 
amounts of the two species have reacted. A secondary indicator is often used to provide a visual clues; 
the indicator will change color at its endpoint. The defining characteristic of a good indicator is one that 
has its endpoint as close to the reaction’s stoichiometric point as possible. In Experiment 4B, a secondary 
indicator is not needed, for the intensely-colored permanganate solution serves as its own indicator 
through a redox reaction. The product Mn2+ has a faint pink color, which serves as the visual indicator 
that the stoichiometric point has been reached.

3. Experimental 
3A. General Information and Materials 
 
 
All pre-synthesized chemicals were obtained from the York 3150 chemistry stockroom. The iron 
oxalate coordination complex was prepared in accordance with the instructions provided in the lab 
manual.1 All reactions were carried out in the York #### laboratory classroom under normal atmospheric 
conditions. The chemicals, listed in order of use, were: Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O, H2O, 6M H2SO4, 1M 
H2C2O4, 2M K2C2O4, 6% H2O2, 95% C2H5OH, 0.01M KMnO4, 1M NaOH, C6H8O6, 0.0020 bipyridyl 
solution, pH 4.7 buffer, 9M H2SO4, 0.5M CaCl2. The major laboratory apparatus used, apart from the 
standard laboratory balances, ovens, and glassware, was the Spectronic 20 spectrophotometer. 
 
 
 
3B. Synthesis of the Iron-Oxalate Coordination Complex 
1.2000g of the hydrated salt Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O (3.0600 x 10-3 mol) were obtained and 
combined with 3 mL of deionized water and 2 drops of 6M H2SO4 in a 50 mL beaker. The solution was 
swirled to dissolve the salt, and heated in a water bath to complete the dissolution. Using a 15 mL 
graduated cylinder, 6 mL of 1M H2C2O4 were measured into the beaker, forming a yellow precipitate. 
The solution pH was confirmed to be strongly acidic, and 6M H2SO4 was added if necessary. A water 
bath was set up and the solution was swirled for several minutes to digest the precipitate, then the beaker 
was removed from the heat and allowed to cool to room temperature. The resulting supernatant was then 
removed using a dropper. The remaining precipitate was washed three times with 3 mL volumes of hot 

 
deionized water, with the waste put into the proper receptacle. 3 mL of 2M K2C2O4 were added and the 
mixture was heated to 40°C. 10 mL of 6% H2O2 were then slowly added dropwise, with constant stirring 
and monitoring of the 40°C solution environment. If there is not a brown solid of Fe2(C2O4)3 at this point, 
more peroxide was added. The mixture was then heated to boiling, removed from heat, and had added to 
it 2 mL of 1M H2C2O4. The solution was then stirred, and 15 drops more of 1M H2C2O4 were added. 
There should have been a yellow-green solution of dissolved Kw[Fex(C2O4)y]·zH2O. If not, 12 drops each 
of 2M K2C2O4 and 1M H2C2O4 were added. If still not yellow-green, several drops of 6% H2O2 were 
added, the solution was heated gently, and further assistance was sought. The product was then 
crystallized by introducing 6 mL of 95% C2H5OH (EtOH). If crystals formed in massive amounts, they 
were redissolved upon warming of the solution. If few crystals formed, 25 drops more of EtOH were 
added to increase crystallization. The beaker was then stored in foil and stored securely to allow for the 
completion of crystallization. At least 36 hours of waiting were required in between procedures. The 
mass of a clean and dried crucible was determined. Using a filtering station setup with a vacuum trap, the 
crystals were filtered, air-dried for a few minutes, and then washed twice with small volumes of 95% 
EtOH. The vacuum was continued until the alcohol odor was gone. The crystals were then allowed to 
air-dry for at least 30 minutes. The mass of the filled crucible was taken, and the mass of the crystals 
calculated. A minimum of 0.5 g of the neon-green, sand-like crystals were necessary for continuation of 
the experiment. 
 
3C. Analysis of the Oxalate Ligand Content through Redox Titration 
 
 
Using weighing paper, four 0.1000 g samples of the synthesized green crystals were obtained and 
distribute among four 125 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. 30 mL of deionized water and 5 mL of 6M H2SO4 were 
added to each flask, which was then swirled to dissolve the solid. Using a clean dry 100 mL beaker, 90 
mL of the standardized KMnO4 solution was obtained and the exact concentration noted. A buret was 
obtained and labeled, and thoroughly cleansed it and checked it for any defects. It was then rinsed twice 
with 5 mL volumes of deionized water and 2 mL volumes of the permanganate solution, then filled to 
approximately the 0.00 mL mark. Reading the top of the meniscus, the initial volume was recorded. One 
of the Erlenmeyer flasks (with the prepared solution) was heated to 60°C and the titration with KMnO4 
began. One “quick-and-dirty” titration was performed to save time and to approximate the volume of 
potassium permanganate needed to react completely with the iron oxalate solution. The titration endpoint 
was reached when the solution turned and stayed pink for at least 30 seconds. The end volume of KMnO4 
was recorded, and the approximate volume of KMnO4 used to complete the titration was calculated. The 
titration steps were then repeated for each of the remaining three flasks. The leftover waste solutions 
were then neutralized and disposed of down the drain. Any permanganate remaining in the buret was 
neutralized and combined it with the oxalate waste in the waste bottle in the fume hood. 
 
3D. Quantitative Analysis of the Iron (III) Ion through Solution Spectrophotometry 
 
 
0.1200g of [Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O] was dissolved in the corresponding volume of deionized 
water in a 100 mL volumetric flask, giving a solution of approximately 0.003M in iron (II). In a 50 mL 
volumetric flask, 50 mg of C6H8O6 and 15 mL of deionized water were combined, and the solution was 
swirled to dissolve the solid. Using a 2 mL pipet, 2 mL of the standard iron solution was transferred from 
the 100 mL flask into the 50 mL flask, along with 15 mL of the 0.0020M bipyridyl solution. The solution 
was then thoroughly mixed by inverting the sealed flask several times. Finally, the dilution was 
completed by filling the 50 mL volumetric flask to its calibration line with pH 4.7 buffer. This solution 
(A) was the “known” which would be used to determine the iron concentration of the complex. The 
solution was then transferred to a clean and dry 125 mL Erlenmeyer flask and sealed. 
 
 
0.1050g of the synthesized green crystals were measured and combined with 20 mL of deionized 
water and 5 mL of 9M H2SO4 in a 50 mL disposable centrifuge tube, swirling to allow the crystals to 
dissolve. 15 mL of 0.5M CaCl2 were then added and the solution was allowed stand for at least 5 minutes 
for complete precipitation. The tube was then centrifuged for 2 minutes in a balanced centrifuge. Using a 
dropper, all the supernatant liquid was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask. The solution was then 

 
diluted to the calibration line with deionized water and mixing thoroughly. The centrifuge tube was then 
recapped and disposed of. 80 mg of solid ascorbic acid, 2 mL of the solution from the 100 mL flask (of 
supernatant & water), 20 mL of the 0.0020M bipyridyl solution, and 20 mL of the pH 4.7 buffer were 
then added to a 50 mL volumetric flask. The final solution pH was checked to be approximately 5.0, and 
more buffer was added if necessary. The solution was then diluted to the calibration line with deionized 
water and thoroughly mixed. This solution (B) was the “unknown” which would be used to determine the 
iron concentration of the complex. This solution was then transferred to a clean and dry 125 mL 
Erlenmeyer flask and sealed.

4A. Synthesis of the Iron-Oxalate Coordination Complex 
Observations 
 
The iron (II) ammonium sulfate, of chemical equation Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2, was a light blue-green 
crystalline substance. Exactly 1.2000g of the hydrated salt was measured out and combined with 3.0 mL 
of deionized H2O and 3 drops of 6M H2SO4, a clear and colorless solution, in a 50 mL beaker. The 
solution was then immersed in a water bath and achieved a maximum heating temperature of 56.6°C, then 
was removed from the heat. 

 
 
5.98 mL of 1M oxalic acid (H2C2O4, a clear and colorless solution) was then introduced into the same 
beaker, immediately producing a yellow precipitate of Fe(C2O4) suspended – and later settling – in a clear 
solution with a goldenrod hue. 
 
Upon testing the solution with pH paper, it was confirmed that the beaker contained “strongly acidic” 
solution. The beaker was then lowered into the same water bath and was allowed to boil for four minutes 
at a maximum temperature of 100°C. The solution was then removed from heat and allowed to cool for 
nine minutes. At this time, the digested precipitate had settled to the bottom of the baker, with a light, 
clear yellow solution on the top. A watch glass was placed over the mouth of the baker to keep out any 
possible impurities and contaminants. The decanted supernatant was a clear green-yellow solution, and a 
soggy mustard-colored heap of solid was left behind in the original beaker. The precipitate was washed 
with ~3 mL samples of water. The decanted liquid ranged from clear yellow to clear and colorless. 
 
The potassium oxalate solution was clear and colorless. After 3.0 mL of it was added to the beaker, 
the contents turned an orangish-yellow. The beaker was heated to 42.0°C, and upon removal from the 
heat, 10 mL of 6% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, a clear and colorless solution) was slowly added. Small 
bubbles appeared with almost every drop, and the beaker turned warm to the touch, indicating the taking 
place of an exothermic reaction. The beaker contents soon turned a rusty, murky brown. The mixture 
was then heated to 100.0°C without noticeable change in appearance, but turned a clear green-yellow 
color upon the addition of 2.0 mL of 1M H2C2O4. 145 drops of 95% ethanol (EtOH) were added, and 
crystallization initiated. The beaker was wrapped in foil and left in the lab drawer for a week. Upon 
return, a layer of small granulated radioactive-green crystals had settled to the bottom of the baker, which 
was also filled with a clear neon-green solution. Vacuum-filtering the crystals produced a clear yellow-
green solution, which turned cloudy after a few minutes. The crystals were left in the crucible for use in 
the next part of the experiment.

5. Discussion 
 
 
4A – Synthesis of an Iron (III) Oxalate Complex 
 
 
In the synthesis of the iron (III) oxalate complex, 1.1###g of the final lime-green crystals were 
obtained. The percent yield of K[Fe3+(C2O4)2]·2H2O was calculated to be 11#.##%, and the percent yield 
of K3[Fe3+(C2O4)3]·3H2O was calculated to be 74.###%. Since the experimental values may not exceed 
100% of the calculated theoretical value (which assumes ideal conditions), the possibility of the complex 
being K[Fe3+(C2O4)2]·2H2O is thrown out, leaving K3[Fe3+(C2O4)3]·3H2O as the correct one. There are no 
averaged values because only one trial was performed. 
 
 
Apart from the usual human-mediated errors in the measuring and distribution of chemicals and 
in the readings of instrumental information – both of which are of much concern in this particular 
experiment, given that the experimental procedure contained little else but sequential measurements and 
readings – another source of error involved the maintenance of the solution pH level. In step two, the pH 
was checked to be strongly acidic. Such an environment would also result in an increased presence of the 
oxalate ion (see equation two, from Introduction), which would then be available for coordination with 
the Fe3+ ion. Had the environment been insufficiently acidic, equation two’s equilibrium would have 
favoured the reactant side, resulting in an excess of oxalic acid (H2C2O4), which is useless for complex 
ion formation. Equation two’s equilibrium is carefully maintained by balancing the stoichiometric 

 
amount of oxalic acid and aqueous potassium oxalate (K2C2O4, which dissolves in solution to give the 
oxalate ion, C2O4
2-). 
 
 
Additional sources of error involved dirty and/or improperly calibrated glassware, failure to 
completely encase the crystals in foil (they’re light-sensitive), improper cleaning of the crucible (which 
would lead to inaccurate filtration), contaminated or already-catalyzed H2O2 (which would result in the 
hindered oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+), and failure to allow the crystals to completely air-dry (mass of H2O 
would have accidentally been included, resulting in an overestimation of the percentage yield). 
 
 
Although the calculated percentage yield of K3[Fe3+(C2O4)3]·3H2O was sufficiently high enough 
to conclude that this experimental procedure is both an effective and successful way to synthesize, isolate, 
and purify an iron (III) oxalate compound, it was noted that the filtrant liquid in step 6 eventually turned 
cloudy and appeared as if there was a minimal sprinkling of crystals in the bottom of the filter flask. 
These crystals may have been lost in the filtration process by the use of the 95% EtOH. Had the EtOH 
been more highly concentrated, the crystals would probably have remained in the crucible, seeing as they 
were water-soluble and may have dissolved in the 5% of H2O present in the ethanol mixture. 
 
 
4B – Analysis of the Oxalate Ligands in the Iron (III) Oxalate Complex 
 
 
In the analysis of the green synthesis product for oxalate content, it was found that the four trials 
yielded calculations of 61#, 60#, 61#, and 61# mmol of C2O4
2- per 100g of complex analyzed. The 
averaged value, with the inclusion of two standard deviations, reported to be 61# ± 7.## mmol of C2O4
2- 
per 100g of complex analyzed. The relative small size of the standard deviations (1.17% of the average) 
suggest that the titrations were performed very precisely with one another, and such a conclusion is 
consistent with the experimental findings as well. The millimoles of oxalate within the crystals were 
.64#, .63#, .62#, and .62#, for trials A-D, respectively. The weight percentages for each trial (A-D) were 
54.0#%, 53.4#%, 54.1#%, and 53.##%, with an average of 53.## ± .5###%. The standard deviation in 
this case was 1.11% of the average value, also indicating that the samples are very precise in 
measurement. No obvious outliers were identified, so the Q-Test was skipped and no extra trials were 
attempted. 
 
 
Possibility for error included the initial mass-recordings of the crystals; even small amounts of 
crystals may have adhered to the weighing paper, and in the transfer of the crystals from the balance to 
the flask, air currents and unsteady movements may have also caused the loss of material. A possible 
remedy for this would be to “mass by difference,” the technique of which was practiced in an earlier 
laboratory experiment. 
 
 
Also, realizing that the buret is a rather large and unwieldy instrument, it may have been very 
possible for some of the permanganate analyte to have been lost in the filling and utilizing of the buret. A 
leaking stopcock would also be a source of error. A very common source of error with burets and 
titration involves overshooting the endpoint – when the endpoint is fast approaching, even the extra 
addition of a drop or two could turn the solution from clear into a permanent pink. The “quick and dirty” 
titration was designed with student impatience in mind; after one “q&d” trial was performed, an 
approximation for the volume of KMnO4 needed could be made, and the rest of the titrations could 
proceed more smoothly. 
 
 
Minor sources of error may have involved: impurity of crystals, improper massing of the crystals, 
dirty and/or contaminated glassware, improperly calibrated instruments, failing to record the initial 
volume of the titrant, inconsistent readings of the buret (i.e., above eye level, below, etc), the interaction 
of the CO2 of the atmosphere on the relative acidity of the analyte (resulting in more KMnO4 used than 
was actually needed), and general calculation errors. 
 
 
Based upon the high precision of the results, it is feasible to suggest that redox titrations are both 
efficient and accurate techniques by which to determine the content of a particular soluble species (here: 
oxalate ion) in a solution. The method is not as time consuming as gravimetric analysis but is much more 
practical, allowing multiple tests to be run in a short amount of time – incentives for any chemist as well 
as student. 
 
 
4C – Analysis of the Iron in the Iron (III) Oxalate Complex 

 
 
 
The concentrations of Fe3+ within the 100 mL volumetric flasks were calculated to be, in order of 
procedure, 3.935 x 10-3 M, 2.391 x 10-3 M, 2.150 x 10-3 M,, 2.280 x 10-3 M,, and 2.600 x 10-3 M, with an 
average of 0.002343M. The millimoles of Fe3+ calculated to be in the initial ~0.1g sample were found to 
be, in unknown samples 3-5, .2150 mmol Fe3+, .2280 mmol Fe3+, and .2600 mmol Fe3+, with an average 
of .2343 ± .0464 mmol Fe3+. The average weight percentages of Fe3+ for samples 3-5 of the unknowns 
were found to be 12.##%, 12.7#%, and 14.##%, with an average weight percentage of 13.09 ± 2.58%. 
The number of millimoles present in 100g of complex were found to be, for unknown samples 3-5: 
215.### mmol Fe3+, 227.### mmol Fe3+, and 259.### mmol Fe3+, with an average of 23#.### ±4#.## 
mmol Fe3+ in every 100g of complex. 
 
 
Firstly, the Beer’s Law graph could only reflect the innermost 3 values of the known dilution, for 
the first and last trials had absorbance values outside the “suggested” values of .2000 to .8000. However, 
a decent trend line was able to be drawn, and throughout the course of the calculations, the elimination of

Likewise, two trials from the unknown had to be eliminated, for the first and second trials had

There were many sources of error in this experiment. First of all, the dilutions themselves were 
prone to many mistakes, with the possibility of adding incorrect volumes to incorrect test tubes very great. 
Incorrectly reading and using the pipet could also be a source of error. If the crystals had not been 
sufficiently dried before use, then an excess of water would affect the final calculation of the amount of 
water within the complex itself. Incorrectly zeroing the Spectronic 20 would have resulted in the 
inconsistency of readings, as would the gradual dirtying of the test tubes. Even though gloves were worn, 
there were still plenty of opportunities for the test tubes to pick up additional dust and grime along the 
duration of the experiment, thereby affecting the absorbency readings. 
 
 
I may have accidentally removed an excess of water from my complex without meaning to; I 
stored my crystals in my dessicator for the better part of an hour before I realized that the dessicant would 
be likely to have a negative effect on the water content of my hydrated iron oxalate salt. In a slightly less 
elaborate possibility, the beaker of crystals may not have been fully capped, and water may simply have 
evaporated out of the holding container. 
 
 
The spectrophotometer readings were not very reliable, as the instruments seem to be a bit 
outdated and had a rather nasty habit of fluctuating in readings, until it was decided that a bit of accuracy 
could be sacrificed in order for everyone in the classroom to finish the procedure on time. Perhaps if 
there were more instruments available, or better attention and vigilance had been applied towards the 
preparation of the solutions, then Spectrophotometry would have resulted in more successful readings. 
The empirical formula was different from that of the “true” compound mentioned in the postlab; had there 
been a better alternate method to determining the iron (III) content within the complex, then the empirical 
formula would have probably been correctly identified and in case of an experiment concerning the 
identification of an unknown, the unknown would have been properly identified as well.
