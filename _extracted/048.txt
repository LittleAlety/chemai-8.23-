ABSTRACT: Potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihydrate is a commonly prepared coordination compound in general or upper-
level chemistry laboratories and can be recycled to grow large single crystals that instructors may display when introducing the
experiment to classes. Showing the crystal’s shape can also be used to brieﬂy introduce students to X-ray crystallography, symmetry,
and unit cell dimensions.
KEYWORDS: First-Year Undergraduate/General, Upper-Division Undergraduate, Laboratory Instruction, Coordination Compounds,
X-ray Crystallography, Inorganic Chemistry, Demonstrations, Crystals/Crystallography
■INTRODUCTION
The preparation of potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihy-
drate is a well-known coordination chemistry experiment used
in general or upper-level chemistry laboratories.1−5 This
material can be synthesized3,4 from Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O
via an iron(II) oxalate intermediate, analyzed for its oxalate5 or
iron6 content in subsequent experiments, or studied for its
photochemical,7,8 equilibrium,9 and X-ray diﬀraction10 proper-
ties.
Yields of the product vary considerably, from 40% to over
80%, depending on the student’s preparative skills. So, for a lab
class of 24 students working in pairs, 50 g or more of product
could be generated as waste which can reach signiﬁcant levels
after several years of classes even if a portion of the material is
used in additional analytical experiments. Rather than
discarding the excess material, we have stored the products
from multiple laboratories and recycled them to grow large
crystals of the iron coordination compound. While the growth
of a large crystal may require several weeks, the result is an
impressive deep emerald-green colored crystal that can be
stored and displayed to students prior to beginning the lab
class. A discussion of the crystal shape and structure of the
compound can also be informative and used to introduce
students to X-ray crystallography, symmetry, and unit cell
dimensions.
■PROCEDURE
The detailed experimental procedure for recycling potassium
tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihydrate and crystal growth is
provided in the Supporting Information. The method used is
slow evaporation of a saturated solution as previously
described in the Journal for growing large crystals of substances
such as alum.11,12 The recrystallization and crystal growth
summarized below was based on 100 g of the student waste
product and may be scaled as needed.
The 100 g of potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihydrate
student waste was ﬁrst recrystallized in water yielding
approximately 74 g of the pure complex. A deposited seed
crystal was collected, suspended in a saturated solution of the
complex, and allowed to grow by slow deposition over several
weeks. After removal from the solution, the single crystal had a
mass of 38 g, or 51% of the original pure iron complex (see
Figure 1). The remaining saturated solution was left to
evaporate to dryness in air, and the solid residue was returned
to the student waste container and used (after another
recrystallization) to grow additional large crystals.
■HAZARDS
Students and instructors should wear safety glasses during the
preparation and recrystallization of potassium tris(oxalato)-
ferrate(III) which is classiﬁed as Class 4 in terms of acute oral
and dermal toxicity.13 The solid may be stored in a sealed
container away from sunlight and, if not recycled, disposed at
an approved waste disposal plant.13 Use gloves when handling
large crystals of the title compound for display. Safety Data
Sheets for oxalic acid14 and potassium oxalate15 should be
consulted when students use these materials to prepare the
complex.
■DISCUSSION
Displaying a large crystal of potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III)
trihydrate prior to student preparation of the complex not only
is impressive but also will encourage students to take extra care
during the preparative procedure since it is possible to produce
similar, but smaller, nicely shaped individual crystals which can
typically grow to lengths of about 5 mm or more, although in
some cases the crystals may be clumped together.

Crystallization is highly dependent on the presence or
absence of impurities. It can be explained during these
experiments that impurities inhibit the growth of crystals,
and therefore, purity is important for generating good-quality
crystals. Since this is a single-component system, the
conditions that produce good-quality crystals can be examined,
as well as the conditions that do not favor crystal growth.
Crystallization occurs at the molecular or atomic level, so it
follows the same symmetry rules as those for atomic and
molecular symmetry. This relationship provides the link
between matter at the macroscopic scale (the pure crystal)
and at the atomic scale (which is normally not accessible to the
students). The arrangement of the components of the crystal
and the symmetry rules can be examined in the class.
One of the authors (P.C.J.) has previously published the X-
ray crystal structure of potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III)
trihydrate;16 a brief description of this complex is provided
below.
While a detailed explanation of X-ray crystallography is
appropriate for upper-level classes, it is generally beyond the
scope of most freshman-level courses. However, general
chemistry textbooks17 and instructors cover the lattice
structures in crystalline solids which may include a description
of the principles and application of the technique along with a
discussion of unit cells. The eﬀects of the conditions in the
crystallization solution can also be explored, and the preferred
size and symmetry can be examined. These concepts may be
brieﬂy introduced when displaying the bulk crystal of
potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihydrate. The morphology
of the crystals is primarily governed by the size and symmetry
of the unit cell. The details of the unit cell are given in Table 1.
The complex crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/
c. The unit cell has two angles that are exactly 90° if viewed
along the a- and c-directions (the α and γ unit cell angles). The
consequence of this is that the unit cell (and the crystal
structure) has to have a perfect 2-fold rotational symmetry
along the β-axis. Any deviation from right angles will disrupt
the rotational symmetry. This symmetry requirement has to be
followed during crystal growth and means that faces are related
by the 2-fold axis. For example, the faces (11̅0) and (1̅1̅0) are
symmetry-related. In the [Fe(C2O4)3]3−anion, the iron(III)
center is distorted from regular octahedral geometry due to the
chelation of the iron centers by the oxalate ligands with O−
Fe−O bond angles from the oxalates about the iron being
closer to 80° rather than the optimal 90° for true octahedral
geometry. Two of the oxalate ligands are bound asymmetrically
to the iron with Fe−O bond lengths of 1.98 and 2.03 Å while
the third is bound more symmetrically with Fe−O bond
lengths of 2.01 and 2.00 Å.
The potassium counterions are either eight- or nine-
coordinate and are bound to oxygen atoms of the oxalate
ions in turn bound to iron and lattice water molecules. The
eight-coordinate potassium ion is dodecahedral and is bound
by two water molecules and six oxygen atoms of the oxalate
ligands. The two nine-coordinate potassium ions have
polyhedra resembling a double trigonal prism and are bound
by two water molecules and seven oxalate oxygen atoms for
one potassium and one water molecule and eight oxalate
oxygen atoms for the other. The [Fe(C2O4)3]3−fragment is
totally encapsulated by 12 potassium ions which cement the
anionic fragments together in a three-dimensional solid where
all but three of the oxygen atoms of the oxalate groups interact
with at least one potassium ion. Further three-dimensional
interactions in the solid state are provided by hydrogen
bonding with only one oxygen atom of the oxalate ions not
bound by a potassium ion or involved in H-bonding.
Aspects of the above structural description could be diﬃcult
for general chemistry students to appreciate since the concepts
of crystal systems and group theory may be only brieﬂy alluded
to, if at all, at the freshman level. Nevertheless, displaying the
large crystal described here could also serve to brieﬂy introduce
and illustrate some of these concepts which students will
encounter in upper-level courses. This might include some
lattice structures in crystalline solids,17 X-ray crystallogra-
phy,18,19 crystal systems,20 or symmetry21 terminology as well
as making a comparison between the unit cell dimensions and
the bulk crystal appearance to help students understand the
shape of the crystals. The crystal could also be displayed to
classes during the solids and liquids section, usually taught in
the ﬁrst half of the general chemistry sequence and may
include some of these topics.
Students will be brieﬂy introduced to the concept of
molecular symmetry during the organic section in general
chemistry so applying this to an actual bulk crystal, rather than
a plastic molecular model, is a natural extension. The instructor
can rotate the crystal through its various axes or planes to
demonstrate symmetry without bewildering students with
advanced group theory details.
Likewise, while the bulk crystal shape may be proportional
to the unit cell, it is instructional to emphasize that the two do
not necessarily adopt identical shapes. For example, for a cubic
cell, the unit cells can multiply along one dimension giving the
bulk appearance of a shoebox-like shape. An analogy could be
drawn between bricks used for masonry construction. While
individual bricks have identical three-dimensional rectangular
block shapes, the ﬁnal bulk structure (a wall or a house) may
have similarly proportion dimensions but will often be diﬀerent
because the unit blocks can be stacked in diﬀerent ways.
Both the student potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III) trihy-
drate crystals prepared during the lab experiment and the
larger recycled crystal described here have shapes that could be
generally described as thin, elongated “shoeboxes”, but how
does that compare to the unit cell? While the dimensions of
the unit cell (reported to be 7.7 Å × 19.9 Å × 10.3 Å16) and
bulk crystal pictured (9 mm × 75 mm × 33 mm = height ×
length × width) both indicate an approximate 2:1 length to
width ratio (which students could easily calculate to prove),
the thickness of the bulk crystal is proportionally less than the

There also exists a relatively simple relationship between the
geometric area of crystal faces and the lengths of the unit cell
edges.22 A crystal that has a short crystallographic axis (for
example, the a-axis in this crystal, see Figure 3) will have an
elongated morphology in this direction.
Qualitatively, this can be visualized by the stacking of crystal
planes along a preferred direction. Preferred stacking gives rise
to smaller repeat distances between the planes and conversely
longer growth vectors (along the direction [100], in this
crystal). The Bravais−Friedel, Donnay−Harker (BFDH)
model can be used to predict the morphology of crystals
using the unit cell information only (for example, the software
WinXMorph, version 1.4.9, and BFDH Morphology in the
Mercury software).
Despite the diﬀerences between potassium tris(oxalato)-
ferrate(III) trihydrate bulk crystals and the unit cell, the
visualization will give students an appreciation of the ordering
of molecules in the crystalline solid state rather than random
packing in amorphous solids.
